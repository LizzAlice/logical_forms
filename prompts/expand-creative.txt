Here is my whole forms file and the plan I approved.

In evidence, a value that does not start with P-, DEF-, ART-, SET- or SEC- is not a form
id: it is a run label, or the entry stated, which marks a limitation that rests on the
author's own knowledge.

Targets: <section ids or form ids>
Text to write: <a methods section, a summary for a general audience, …>
Mode: creative

Write the text the plan describes. Report only forms the plan lists.

The text has two kinds of sentence:
- A carrying sentence says something the forms say and cites their ids in square
  brackets: "... 33 of 40 executed [P-exec-rate]." One sentence may carry several
  forms, and one form may be spread over several sentences.
- A link sentence helps the reader along: it announces what comes next, refers back to
  something the text already said, or marks a change of topic. Mark it [link] in place
  of an id. It must not state or imply anything that a form could support or
  contradict: no number, no outcome, no cause, no comparison, no judgement.
This is a creative rendering. Its purpose is to get the gist across: what was found,
how sure it is and what is still open.

Report the claims, limitations, assumptions and open questions the plan lists. A result
or setup appears only where one of those needs it or the plan lists it.

There is a third kind of sentence: a framing sentence, invented for the story and
marked [frame]. It gives the setting, the characters and their manner. It must not
state or imply anything that a form could support or contradict: no number, no
outcome, no cause, no judgement about anything the forms describe.

Gist. A carrying sentence may say less than the form:
- You may leave out numbers, paths, versions and ids. Never round or approximate: give
  a number as the form gives it or not at all.
- Make a qualitative statement (a comparison, a direction, "higher", "most", "small")
  only where a form states it in those terms. Do not read a comparison or trend off
  results yourself.
- The forms must say everything the sentence says. The sentence may leave things out,
  but must not turn a hedged or limited statement into a plain one.

Stand-ins. You may replace real things by stand-ins. Begin the draft with a mapping
table: stand-in | stands for (form id or term). One stand-in per thing and one thing
per stand-in, throughout. What a stand-in does or undergoes in a carrying sentence is
what the forms say of the thing it stands for, and the sentence cites those forms.
Framing sentences may give a stand-in looks, a voice and a mood, but no acts or
fortunes that would say something about the thing it stands for. A stand-in may not be
a string from an avoid list.

Rules 2 and 6 hold as they are; a scene is a passage.

Do not put right what the forms leave wrong or unfinished. An open question stays
unanswered. A limitation stays in force and is not overcome in the plot. A refuted form
stays wrong. A preliminary claim or limitation stays uncertain. A weak or failed result is
not turned into a success. The ending resolves nothing that the forms do not resolve.

A passage is the text under one top-level heading with its subsections; without
headings, a paragraph. An abstract or summary is a passage of its own.

Shape. Follow the plan's headings and tables. Open a results part with what was found,
in the words of a claim where there is one, then give the numbers. Put parallel things
in the planned tables and do not repeat every cell in prose. Method prose says what
happens in which order. The abstract says why, what was done and the main findings,
with no setup values, within the planned length. Shape never adds content: where the
forms give too little for the text to hang together, keep it short there and list the
gap under "Proposed forms".

Rules:
1. Every sentence is a carrying sentence or a link sentence. A carrying sentence can
   state the statement of a result, claim, assumption, limitation or open, a result's
   scope, an assumption's basis or breaks_if, the definition of a def, or a field
   value of an artifact or setup (a path, a version, a component value), and it cites
   that form's id. Never expand or quote a note field.
2. Register follows status. For results, claims, assumptions, limitations and opens:
   - established → plain assertion
   - preliminary → hedged. A preliminary claim also needs the scope of the results
     under it, in the sentence or earlier in the same passage
   - assumed → explicitly marked as an assumption. The sentence may give the
     assumption's basis and breaks_if, and adds nothing beyond them. State an
     assumption in every text that reports a claim resting on it, directly or through
     other claims. The exception is surface: no: do not write such an assumption out,
     but it still limits what the claims resting on it may assert
   - unsourced → always with a visible marker: "[CITATION NEEDED: <id>]". A sentence
     that reports a claim resting on an unsourced one, directly or through other
     claims, carries the same marker with the id of the unsourced form
   - open → open question or future work only, never a finding
   - closed → not written. Do not pull in the forms that answer it
   - refuted → not written at all, except as an explicit correction
   - outdated → never written as a finding about the current work. Report it only as
     what held for the earlier setup or artifact version, named as such
   - resolved → written only as the hedge on a result or claim it covers (rule 6), in
     the past tense
   For artifacts, setups and defs, which a sentence can name or use:
   - current, and established for a def → named and used normally
   - superseded → named only as the earlier version that a cited result was produced
     with
   - planned → future work only
   - retired → not used; a retired def no longer governs wording
   Sections are never written out.
3. Copy numbers, paths, versions and names verbatim. Never round, reformat, convert or
   spell out: 10 stays 10.
4. A sentence must not exceed the scope of the forms it rests on, and must not assert
   anything listed in their not_supported. For a claim, the governing scope is that of
   the results underneath it, followed transitively through derived_from. A claim with
   no result underneath it has no such scope: stay within its statement and its
   not_supported.
5. Terms follow def: call the thing a def's term names by a string from use, never by
   one from avoid. An avoid string is only forbidden as a name for that thing, and not
   inside a string from use. Ignore defs with status retired.
6. A limitation covers the forms named in its affects and every form that rests on one
   of them, through any number of steps: a claim rests on the forms in its
   derived_from, a result on the forms in its evidence, a setup on the forms in its
   includes and components. Sections play no part. A refuted limitation covers nothing.
   State a limitation once in every passage that reports a result or claim it covers,
   at or before the first such sentence, and make its reach clear ("all scores in this
   section"). Do not repeat it later in the passage. A limitation with severity: major
   gets a sentence of its own in each such passage; others may share a sentence. A
   sentence that only states a value of a setup or artifact needs no hedge.
   In an abstract or summary of a text with a body: state every major limitation
   that covers something it reports; a minor one may be left out there if the body
   states it. A limitation without severity is minor. Keep every hedge and scope
   that rule 2 asks for.
7. Add nothing that is not in a form. If the text needs a bridging fact or a reason
   that no form gives, leave that sentence out, continue, and list a proposed new form
   under "Proposed forms" after the text.
8. Do not correct, complete or improve what the forms say, even where a form looks
   wrong or contradicts what you know. Write what the form says, and list the form's
   id with your reason under "Possible errors" after the text.
9. Wording. Change the structure, keep the words that carry the meaning.
   Free: the order of forms and clauses, sentence boundaries, merging several forms
   into one sentence or spreading one over several, active or passive voice, the
   grammatical person, pronouns for things already named.
   Not to change: numbers, paths, versions, names and def terms; the content words of
   a statement (its verb, and the nouns and adjectives that say what it is about: no
   synonyms, so "scored highest" does not become "performed best"); the small words
   that set its reach (all, none, 4 of 7, not, only, at least, at most, alone or tied,
   comparatives and superlatives, may, must, and qualifiers such as "recorded as");
   the past tense of a result.
   The form must say everything your sentence says, and your sentence must not leave
   out a part of the form whose absence changes what a reader takes away.
   When one sentence carries several forms, or sums up forms already reported, each
   form's statement stays complete, with every part that sets its reach. A short
   label for a form ("the doubled gain") must pass the same test. If the complete
   statements do not fit into one sentence, use several.
   Copy a form that has wording: fixed word for word.
10. Relations. You may say how two forms relate only where a field records it, and you
    then cite both: derived_from (the claim follows from or rests on those forms;
    hedged for a preliminary claim: "suggests", not "shows"), evidence (measured
    with), follows (runs after), uses (calls), includes (ran together), affects (the
    limitation applies to), resolved_by, replaced_by, answered_by. State no other
    relation. If the text needs one, propose a claim (rule 7).
    Check these three against the fields before you write them:
    - words of reason and consequence: so, therefore, thus, hence, because, since, as
      a result, which is why, despite, although. Also inside one form: where the
      statement has a semicolon or "and" between its halves, do not write "so";
    - "this suggests", "this shows", "it follows": "this" points at the sentence
      before, which must report a form in the claim's derived_from. Where the claim
      rests on several forms, name them all;
    - an opening "So" or "therefore" before a step ("So we repeated the scoring")
      gives a reason for the step. "Then" and "next" give only an order.
    "And", "also", "then" and a full stop state no relation and are always safe.
11. Tables. Every rule applies to a table. Give each row a last column "forms" with
    the ids it reports. Row label, column header and cell together must say what the
    form says (rule 9): the header carries what the column's cells share, the row
    label what the row's cells share, the cell the rest, including a qualifier that
    holds for that cell only. Numbers and names verbatim. Fill no cell that no form
    fills; compute, average or carry over nothing. The caption says what rows and
    columns are. A limitation that covers a form in the table is stated in the same
    passage at or before the table, or in the caption.
